# Edward-Widerberg-Dissertation

## Do Clubs Take Financial Risks to Secure Promotion or Avoid Relegation?
### Evidence from the EFL Championship and 2. Bundesliga

## Abstract

European second-tier football clubs routinely overspend on player wages relative to revenue, a behaviour Evans, Walters, and Hamil (2022) term "financial gambling." This project tests whether that pattern, first documented in England's EFL Championship, also holds in Germany's 2. Bundesliga — a league with a smaller promotion-relegation revenue gap and the near-universal 50+1 member-ownership model.

Using club financial statements from 2021/22 to 2023/24 (Companies House for English clubs; Bundesanzeiger and DFL reports for German clubs), each club-season is classified as a "gambler" if wage growth outpaces both revenue and profit growth, then evaluated against a proposition-based payoff matrix tied to each club's prior league position.

**The headline result:** gambling is roughly twice as common in the Championship (30% of 53 club-seasons) as in the 2. Bundesliga (17% of 35 club-seasons) — consistent with Championship clubs clustering well above UEFA's 70% wage-to-turnover sustainability threshold, while nearly all 2. Bundesliga clubs stay below it.

The more significant finding is *where* that gambling concentrates. In the Championship, it clusters among promotion-chasing clubs (67% of clubs finishing 3rd–6th the prior season gambled, and gambling clubs clearly outperformed non-gamblers). In the 2. Bundesliga, the pattern inverts: mid-table clubs never gamble, and gambling instead concentrates almost exclusively among clubs fighting relegation (44%), where it reliably succeeds. This suggests financial gambling isn't just a function of the promotion incentive — it's shaped by ownership structure, revenue distribution, and the severity of relegation in each system.

## Key findings

- **Prevalence:** 30% of Championship club-seasons vs. 17% of 2. Bundesliga club-seasons were classified as financial gamblers.
- **English pattern:** gambling concentrates among promotion-chasing clubs (Proposition I, 3rd–6th place) — 67% gambled, and every non-gambling club in that group regressed or was relegated.
- **German pattern (inverted):** zero gambling among mid-table clubs; gambling concentrates among relegation-threatened clubs (44%), all of whom achieved their objective of survival.
- **Regulatory implication:** the EFL's Profitability and Sustainability Rules show no evidence of curbing gambling behaviour, supporting Evans et al.'s "regulatory legitimacy" thesis — rules that confer legitimacy on overspending rather than constrain it. The DFL's licensing regime, ownership model, and narrower promotion incentive appear jointly more effective, though the analysis can't cleanly separate which factor does the most work.

## Methodology

A three-part classifier adapted from Evans et al. (2022): a club-season is a "gambler" only if wage expenditure (1) rose year-on-year, (2) rose more than revenue, and (3) rose more than the change in profit. Classified gamblers are then plotted on a payoff matrix against their prior-season finishing position (grouped into league-specific "propositions" reflecting realistic ambition — promotion push, mid-table, or relegation battle) and their outcome the following season, to assess whether the gamble paid off.

## Data sources

- English clubs: statutory accounts filed with Companies House (UK)
- German clubs: Bundesanzeiger filings and DFL Deutsche Fußball Liga economic reports
- League positions: Transfermarkt
- Seasons covered: 2021/22–2023/24 (EFL Championship: 53 club-seasons; 2. Bundesliga: 35 club-seasons)
